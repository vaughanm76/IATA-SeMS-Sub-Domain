      The rule base keeps growing. Implementation stopped following it.

      Annex 17 has been amended nineteen times and reissued in twelve editions. Global effective implementation climbed steeply through to 2017 — and has been flat ever since, across two further editions and three further amendments. What that gap exposes is not a shortage of standards. It is the capacity to manage security against them.

      30
      Editions and amendments of Annex 17 issued since 1974. A nineteenth amendment is pending.
      +18.0
      Percentage points of global effective implementation gained between 2008 and 2017.
      −0.3
      Points moved between 2017 and 2023, across two editions and three amendments.
      ≈72%
      The level global effective implementation has held at for the better part of a decade.
      The binding constraint is capability, not coverage More prescription is no longer converting into more implementation. A security management system is what turns a standard into something an organisation can actually run, evidence and improve.

      Read this carefully before using it. USAP‑CMA effective implementation measures a State's oversight capability, not an operator's security outcomes. The protocol question set also expands as the Annex grows, so States are re-measured against a harder test each cycle — holding roughly 72% against a rising bar is not the same thing as standing still.

      That qualification arguably reinforces the point rather than undermining it: the yardstick keeps moving because the rule base keeps growing, faster than States and operators can absorb it. The acts-of-unlawful-interference series is indicative, digitised from the source chart, and should be replaced with the underlying ICAO series before publication.

      Two routes. Only one of them moved.

      SeMS was put to ICAO as a standard and did not get there. What happened instead is that the industry built it — through IOSA, through manuals, training and certification, and through a community that now runs to four figures. Twenty-four years of that history, split by which route each step took.

      ICAO route Standard-setting through Annex 17
      Industry route IOSA, IATA guidance, tools and community
      One binding step in twenty-four years, and it came from IOSA 2007 made SeMS an ISM standard for airlines. The 2024 extension to service providers is a recommended practice, so the tier that performs most of the measures is addressed without being bound. Nothing here was ever inherited down the regulatory cascade the way the mandated baseline is — which is why SeMS maturity varies so widely by operator type.

      Rule and risk, inside one structure

      A SeMS does not replace the mandated baseline. It holds the baseline and the risk-based measures in the same structure, so that both answer to one accountable outcome — and so that assurance evidence feeds back into how oversight is targeted.

      Oversight

      Who checks whom

      Each tier verifies the one below it, with depth set by risk rather than by the calendar.

      • ICAO USAP audits the State
      • State oversees the operator
      • Operator oversees its service providers
      Rule-based

      The mandated baseline

      Non-negotiable, inherited downward, and identical for everyone in the same category.

      • Annex 17 SARPs
      • Regional regulations, where they apply
      • National regulations and directives
      • Operator programme — AOSP / SSP
      Risk-based

      Measures beyond the baseline

      Threat-informed, specific to your operation, and revised when the picture changes.

      • Threat and vulnerability assessment
      • Measures proportionate to the risk identified
      • Assurance monitoring and SeKPIs
